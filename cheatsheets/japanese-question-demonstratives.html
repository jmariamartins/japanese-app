疑問詞・指示詞
Question Words & Demonstratives — Reference Guide
Question Words Gimonshi Used to ask who, what, how, where, when, why...
Word Use Case Example Sentence
nani what Asking "what" — before a particle Used when "what" comes before a particle like wo, ga, de Nani wo tabemasu ka? What are you eating?
nan what Asking "what" — before desu / numbers Same word! "Nan" is used directly before desu, or with counters like -sai, -mei, -ji Nan desu ka? What is it?
With counters nan + counter = asking about quantity/age/time Nan-sai desu ka? How old are you? (lit. What age?)
Asking "what kind of" (no) nan no + noun = what kind of / what [noun] Kore wa nan no hon desu ka? What kind of book is this?
dou — how / what about
dou how / what about Asking about condition or state "How is it?" / "What do you think?" — very common in daily conversation Kore wa dou desu ka? How is this? / What do you think of this?
Offering or suggesting something "How about...?" — used to make polite suggestions Koohii wa dou desu ka? How about a coffee?
Asking how something is done Combined with shimasu to ask the method Dou shimasu ka? What will you do? / How do you do it?
Other essential question words — for reference
dare who Asking about a person "Donata" is the polite version of dare Kare wa dare desu ka? Who is he?
doko where Asking about a place Used with location particles ni, e, de, kara Doko kara kimashita ka? Where are you from?
itsu when Asking about time Unlike specific times (e.g. 7 o'clock), itsu does NOT take ni Itsu kimasu ka? When are you coming?
naze why Asking for a reason "Doushite" is a softer / more conversational alternative Naze nihongo wo manabimasu ka? Why are you learning Japanese?
Demonstratives — The Ko-So-A-Do System Shijishi Japanese has a 3-distance system: near me → near you → far from both → question
Japanese demonstratives follow a beautiful pattern called ko-so-a-do: words starting with ko- = near the speaker, so- = near the listener, a- = far from both, do- = question form (which one?). Once you learn one row, the pattern applies to all!
Meaning / Type ko — near me so — near you a — far from both do — which? (question)
Thing this / that / which korethis (thing) sorethat (near you) arethat over there dorewhich one?
Place here / there / where kokohere sokothere (near you) asokoover there dokowhere?
Direction this way / that way kochirathis way / this person (polite) sochirathat way (near you) achirathat way (far) dochirawhich way? (also polite "where?")
Noun modifier this [noun] / that [noun] konothis + noun sonothat + noun (near you) anothat + noun (far) donowhich + noun?
Type / Kind this kind / what kind konnathis kind of sonnathat kind of annathat kind of (far) donnawhat kind of?
Example sentences
Word Use Case Example Sentence
korethis (thing) Referring to something near the speaker Stands alone — does NOT attach to a noun (use "kono" for that) Kore wa nan desu ka? What is this?
sorethat (near you) Referring to something near the listener Used when the object is closer to the person you're speaking to Sore wa eigo no shinbun desu. That is an English newspaper.
arethat (far away) Referring to something far from both speaker and listener Also used for shared memories: "that thing we both know about" Are wa nan desu ka? What is that (over there)?
konothis [noun] Modifying a noun — always followed by a noun kono/sono/ano always attach directly to a noun, unlike kore/sore/are Kono hon wa omoshiroi desu. This book is interesting.
anothat [noun] (far) Modifier for a distant noun — also used warmly for known people You already know this one! "ano onnanoko" = that girl (from your Duolingo lesson) Ano onnanoko no namae wa nan desu ka? What is that girl's name?
douhow / what about Asking how something is / offering something "Dou desu ka" is one of the most useful phrases in daily Japanese Tokyo wa dou desu ka? How is Tokyo? / What do you think of Tokyo?
Key distinction — kore vs kono: "Kore" stands alone as a pronoun ("this is..."), while "kono" must be followed by a noun ("this [noun] is..."). So: Kore wa hon desu (This is a book) vs Kono hon wa... (This book is...). Same rule applies across the whole system: kore/sore/are/dore = standalone, kono/sono/ano/dono = before a noun.
Tip: The ko-so-a-do pattern is one of the most elegant systems in Japanese — once memorised, it unlocks dozens of words at once!
