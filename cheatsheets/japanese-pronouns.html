Pronoun Meaning & Register Notes Example
— First Person — I / We —
watashi
I 		neutral polite The standard "I" for most situations — safe for everyone to use Default choice for learners. Used by all genders in formal and everyday speech Watashi wa Maria desu. I am Maria.
watakushi
I 		very formal The most formal "I" — used in business, speeches, official settings Rarely used in daily conversation. Signals extreme politeness or professionalism Watakushi wa kaisha no shacho desu. I am the company president.
boku
I 		masculine casual Soft, friendly masculine "I" — used by boys, young men, and male anime characters Sounds polite compared to "ore". Occasionally used by women in casual speech or song lyrics Boku wa nihongo wo manabiimasu. I am learning Japanese.
ore
I 		masculine rough/casual Very masculine, assertive "I" — common among adult men in casual settings Sounds rude in formal situations. Very common in everyday male speech and manga/anime Ore wa pan wo tabete imasu. I am eating bread.
uchi
I 		feminine casual Feminine casual "I" — common in Kansai (Osaka/Kyoto) dialect and among young women Literally means "home/inside" — reflects the Japanese idea of self as part of an in-group Uchi wa koohii ga suki. I like coffee. (casual)
— Second Person — You —
anata
you 		neutral use with care Technically the standard "you" but often sounds cold or confrontational Native speakers usually avoid this — they use the person's name instead. Wives sometimes use it affectionately for their husbands Anata wa nihongo wo hanashimasu ka? Do you speak Japanese?
anta
you 		casual can sound blunt Contracted form of anata — casual and direct, can sound dismissive Used in arguments or very casual speech. Best avoided by learners until you know the relationship well Anta wa doko ni ikimasu ka? Where are you going?
kimi
you 		masculine tendency casual Affectionate or superior-to-subordinate "you" — used by men to close friends, partners, or juniors Common in song lyrics and romantic speech. Sounds warm when used appropriately Kimi wa eigo ga jouzu desu ne. Your English is good, isn't it!
omae
you 		masculine rough Very blunt "you" — used between close male friends or to assert dominance Can be offensive to strangers. Very common in manga and anime, but risky in real life Omae wa nani wo shite imasu ka? What are you doing? (blunt)
— Third Person — He / She / They —
kare
he / boyfriend 		neutral Means both "he" and "boyfriend" depending on context "Kare ga imasu" = "I have a boyfriend" — same word! Japanese often uses names or roles (sensei, okaasan) instead Kare wa pan wo tabete imasu. He is eating bread.
kanojo
she / girlfriend 		neutral Means both "she" and "girlfriend" depending on context "Kanojo ga imasu" = "I have a girlfriend". Like kare, often replaced by names in natural speech Kanojo wa eiga wo mite imasu. She is watching a film.
karera
they 		neutral Plural "they" — karera is used for male or mixed groups "Kanojotachi" = they (female group). In practice, Japanese often avoids third-person pronouns entirely and just repeats the name or uses context Karera wa nihon kara kimashita. They came from Japan.
The -tachi suffix — making pronouns plural Plural
-tachi is added to pronouns and people-words to make them plural. It can also be added to a person's name to mean "that person and their group". For example: Maria-tachi = Maria and her friends/group.
Singular + tachi = Plural Meaning
watashi watashitachi we
anata anatatachi you all
kare karera they (male / mixed)
kanojo kanojotachi they (female group)
boku bokutachi we (masculine, casual)
Maria Maria-tachi Maria and her group
Why Japanese avoids pronouns
Unlike English, Japanese speakers rarely use "you" or "he/she" in conversation. Instead they use:
— The person's name: "Maria wa doko ni ikimasu ka?" (Where is Maria going?)
— Their title or role: sensei (teacher), okaasan (mother), shacho (company president)
— Simply dropping the subject when context makes it clear

Using "anata" too often is one of the most common mistakes learners make — it's technically correct but sounds unnatural or even confrontational to native speakers!
Tip: As a learner, stick to watashi (I) and use names instead of "you" — you will sound natural immediately!
